# The AI Infrastructure Signal · Issue #48 · November 22nd to November 28th, 2026

## Issue #48 — November 22nd to November 28th, 2026

### Executive Summary
The week was defined by a sharp correction in the semiconductor sector as major investors like David Tepper reduced positions in AMD and Broadcom while piling into NVIDIA at lower valuations. Simultaneously, geopolitical friction intensified with new US export controls halting shipments to Southeast Asia and Nvidia establishing a new oversight committee to clamp down on compliance risks. Despite these headwinds, specific segments showed resilience: AWS Trainium3 outperformed the NVIDIA GB300 in inference benchmarks, and Microsoft's Coherent partnership generated significant value for Nvidia. The market is navigating a tension between macroeconomic fears regarding China and regulatory crackdowns versus tangible progress in HPC efficiency and quantum readiness.

### Signal of the Week
Nvidia’s manufacturing partners halted server shipments to Southeast Asia following new US chip export controls, triggering an immediate 8% drop in Nvidia stock alongside significant declines for AMD and Broadcom. This development is corroborated by reports detailing the halt in AI server production and Jefferies estimates that these restrictions have already cost Nvidia over $44 billion in potential revenue. For the investment outlook, this signals a structural shift where growth is increasingly constrained by geopolitical boundaries rather than just technological bottlenecks, forcing investors to re-evaluate supply chain exposure in Asia-Pacific regions while looking toward domestic US manufacturing and non-compliant markets for future demand.

### Market Pulse
The market is currently navigating a complex cycle where geopolitical tensions and regulatory crackdowns are acting as a headwind to short-term momentum, even as long-term demand for AI infrastructure remains structurally intact. The dominant tension this week is the clash between the need for aggressive compute scaling and the reality of shrinking supply chain access in Southeast Asia and China; Nvidia's new oversight committee and halted shipments to SEA are concrete examples of this friction. I'm confident on the thesis that domestic US manufacturing and alternative markets will fill the gap, but early call, as we must wait to see if these restrictions harden into permanent barriers. The sector is rotating from pure compute hype toward efficiency plays like memory consolidation and networking upgrades, where the investment case is clearer despite the stock price volatility.

The core theses driving this week's strategy are: first, that Nvidia remains the primary beneficiary of a "friend-shoring" trend as its domestic partners secure revenue streams in compliant markets; second, that memory stocks like Micron and SK Hynix offer better risk-adjusted returns than pure-play GPU manufacturers given their exemption status from some new controls; third, that networking infrastructure is entering a maturation phase where operators like T-Mobile and BT are testing AI-RAN economics ahead of 6G deployment. I'm betting on the resilience of the compute stack but will be more selective on exposure to regions facing export bans.

Two specific risks could break this bullish thesis: first, if the US government expands the Southeast Asia ban to include other critical components or allies, which would sever the primary supply chain for Nvidia and AMD; second, if a major cloud provider like Microsoft delays its HPC expansion due to financing issues, creating a ripple effect that slows down the entire ecosystem's adoption of new chips. The single most important thing to watch next week is whether any further announcements come from the US Commerce Department regarding the scope of the chip export controls, as this will dictate the immediate direction of the semiconductor indices.
